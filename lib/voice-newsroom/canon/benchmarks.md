# He Wanted Them to Know He Knew.
Source: https://github.com/codychin/league-of-ordinary-gentlemen/blob/3820ad979ef3b91929a1d54c708eed49c3e231d2/app/articles/data.js
Key: marnie-corgi-he-wanted-them-to-know

There is a photograph from the Seahawks’ corgi race that should be hung in every youth sports facility in America, then covered with a sheet whenever the children arrive.

In it, a corgi in navy has established enough separation from his competitors to turn his head and look back. Behind him, two other dogs are still engaged in the original activity: running as fast as their little legs will carry them. They have dressed for the occasion. They are making an effort. Somewhere nearby, people who love them are presumably saying their names.

The dog in front has decided to check on all that.

You can forgive speed. Some dogs are fast. You cannot account for every variable in a field of animals whose preparation may include eating a receipt. What requires explanation is the discretionary use of the neck. Every fraction of a second spent looking backward is a fraction of a second this animal believes he can afford. He has reviewed the competition and incorporated sightseeing into his race plan.

There is a Usain Bolt quality to the image, except Bolt had the decency to possess legs. This is a throw pillow with access to a higher gear. His undercarriage is practically brushing the venue, and he has still found time to turn around like a man who has just said something unforgivable at a dinner party and wants to see whether it landed.

[object Object]

Consider the social contract under which the other dogs entered this event. A car ride. Some excitement. A brief athletic obligation, followed by extravagant praise. Nobody explained that they might become supporting characters in another corgi’s Jordan documentary. Their owners signed up for enrichment. They are leaving with film to study.

And now they have to go home together.

That is the part the highlight economy never considers. The losing corgi still has to sit in the back seat while someone up front says, “You did so good,” with the strained brightness of a parent leaving a disastrous school production. The dog does not know what a participation trophy is, but he knows the car is quieter than it was on the way there. At a red light, his owner watches the clip again.

The winner, meanwhile, will be impossible at the dog park. You cannot return an animal to ordinary society after he has discovered that his physical gifts come with a broadcast angle. A tennis ball will no longer be enough. He will need the tennis ball thrown in front of people whose opinion matters to him. Fetch will require a media availability.

Naturally, there are questions about the program. Everyone else appears to have brought a pet. Somebody brought a system. I am not accusing this dog of anything, but I would like to understand his relationship with the person who measures his food. We are being asked to accept that this level of separation is available through ordinary kibble and a supportive home environment, and I think the sport deserves a more rigorous conversation.

Still, the legs are only part of the story. The photograph is worth keeping because it catches the moment a wholesome halftime diversion acquires an author. Until that glance, the trailing dogs are simply losing a race. Once he looks back, he is watching them lose it.

Print it wide. Keep the other two in the frame. The empty turf between them is essential to the composition, as is the terrible sincerity of their pursuit. They are still giving everything. He has enough left over to take an interest.

Tonight, all three will be told they are good boys. Two will need to hear it.

# The Juice Box Guy
Source: https://github.com/codychin/league-of-ordinary-gentlemen/blob/3820ad979ef3b91929a1d54c708eed49c3e231d2/app/articles/data.js
Key: hollis-mike-ditka-juice-box-guy

There are indignities in the life of a great man that history has a responsibility to correct.

In 2005, Mike Ditka, a Pro Football Hall of Famer, Super Bowl champion, and one of the most recognizable figures in the history of Chicago sports, was publicly instructed to retrieve a juice box by a man who owned a vitamin store.

The man was Phil Weston. He was played by Will Ferrell. And for a brief, terrible moment in American cinema, the architect of the 1985 Chicago Bears was reduced to an unpaid beverage attendant for a children's soccer team.

Ditka was incredulous.

DO YOU KNOW WHO YOU'RE TALKING TO?

Weston did.

I'm talkin' to the juice box guy.

[object Object]

What followed was a confrontation of astonishing comic proportions. Ditka protested, Ferrell escalated, and the two men descended into an argument that would have been embarrassing for either participant had one of them not been Mike Ditka.

Ferrell was on home turf. This was his movie, his particular brand of absurdity, a world constructed around his considerable comic instincts. Ditka was a retired football coach with no formal acting credentials, standing on a children's soccer field.

And yet, watching the two men argue, it was difficult to determine which one had wandered into the other's profession.

Ferrell had a script, an absurd premise, and the considerable advantage of being the film's protagonist.

Ditka wore a sweater and jeans.

Ordinarily, the athlete's cameo is an exercise in controlled humiliation. A six-foot-five linebacker gets frightened by a house cat. A retired quarterback struggles to operate a household appliance. The imposing man demonstrates his sense of humor by temporarily surrendering the qualities that made him imposing.

Ditka surrendered nothing.

He could be handed the most ridiculous dialogue imaginable and deliver it with the conviction of a man explaining why somebody needed to be blocked harder. Even when the joke was explicitly at his expense, he seemed less concerned with being funny than with the troubling possibility that everyone around him was an idiot.

The sunglasses. The mustache. The sweater and jeans. The almost theological belief in toughness. A voice capable of making an ordinary disagreement sound like the opening minutes of a hostage negotiation.

He had spent decades developing a character so complete that Hollywood could scarcely improve upon it.

*Saturday Night Live* understood this long before the movies did. The Super Fans constructed an entire religion around Ditka. Grown men suffered cardiac episodes contemplating his greatness. The question of whether Ditka could defeat God became a legitimate subject of barroom debate, complicated only by the possibility that Ditka was, in fact, God.

The comedians could invent increasingly impossible feats of strength, but the mustache, the sunglasses, the voice, the sheer gravitational pull of the man required no embellishment.

The actual Mike Ditka was a Hall of Fame tight end, a championship-winning coach, a volcanic television personality, and someone who looked perpetually capable of firing you from a restaurant he did not own.

He was also apparently quite happy to participate in the joke.

The impression of Ditka had become a shared American possession, something people could imitate, exaggerate, argue about, and love without ever needing to explain the relationship. The caricature followed him from football broadcasts to sketch comedy to the occasional Hollywood production, growing increasingly ridiculous without ever becoming particularly difficult to recognize.

The man himself was entitled to a life beyond the character. What the rest of us knew was the mustache, the voice, the sideline eruptions, and the extraordinary ease with which his presence could transform an otherwise ordinary scene.

You could impersonate him, parody him, or put him in a children's movie and ask him to fetch a juice box.

He remained unmistakably Mike Ditka.

Which is why, even as Ferrell's character shouted after him, even as the children were instructed to wave goodbye, it was impossible to watch Ditka leave that soccer field and conclude that the smaller man had won.

Ditka had merely decided he had better things to do.

Mike Ditka died Friday at 86.

Chicago will remember its coach. Football will remember the man who helped transform the tight end position, then built one of the most fearsome teams the sport has ever seen. His players will remember the leader they knew. His family and friends will remember the man they loved.

The rest of us have the extraordinary character he left in public view.

Some knew Ditka as the man who brought a championship to Chicago. Others met him decades later, as a furious old man in a children's soccer movie, or as the subject of a *Saturday Night Live* sketch in which his physical capabilities were compared favorably with those of God.

They inherited the character without necessarily knowing the accomplishments that had made him famous.

And somehow, it was still the same man.

Most famous people eventually become photographs from another time. The clothes look strange. The references require explanation. The achievements grow distant, even when history insists they were important.

Ditka had the peculiar advantage of being impossible to confuse with anyone else.

There will be people who remember exactly where they were when the Bears won Super Bowl XX. There will be others who remember sitting on the living room floor, laughing at a man in a sweater and jeans refusing to get Will Ferrell a juice box.

They may never have seen him lift the Lombardi Trophy. They may know almost nothing about the football career that made all of this possible.

They'll know exactly who they're looking at.

The great ones find ways to belong to more than one generation.

Some leave us trophies. Some leave us a city that will never quite stop talking about them. A few become so thoroughly themselves that even the people who arrive late somehow feel they've known them forever.

And years from now, someone will put on that ridiculous movie. A child will watch a furious man with a mustache argue about a juice box, and someone older will recognize a voice they haven't heard in years.

They'll laugh together.

Rest easy, Coach.

# Sunday at the Maracanã
Source: https://github.com/codychin/league-of-ordinary-gentlemen/blob/3820ad979ef3b91929a1d54c708eed49c3e231d2/app/articles/data.js
Key: sabine-sunday-maracana

RIO DE JANEIRO — There are buildings that belong to whoever owns them, and then there are buildings that have been around long enough to develop more complicated arrangements.

The Maracanã is the latter.

On Sunday afternoon, the Baltimore Ravens and Dallas Cowboys will play the first NFL game ever staged in Rio de Janeiro. The league has spent the preceding week doing what leagues do when arriving somewhere important: installing signage, producing merchandise, arranging appearances, displaying the Lombardi Trophy and all 60 Super Bowl rings, and generally behaving as though a city has been waiting patiently for its activation calendar.

Rio, characteristically, had other things going on.

The grass is bad.

This is not metaphor. Not yet.

The Maracanã pitch has been criticized for months, including publicly by Flamengo and Fluminense players. Repairs were carried out in August. More repairs were planned in September specifically around the NFL game. The stadium hosted 73 matches in 2025, and its administration says the volume of use, combined with weather, has left the field under constant strain.

A full replacement is planned for the end of the year.

Until then, everyone would still quite like to use it.

This is where the Maracanã becomes more interesting than Ravens-Cowboys.

Flamengo and Fluminense administer the stadium under a long-term concession from the state of Rio de Janeiro. Vasco da Gama would also like access to it. Its supporters spent part of this week hanging protest banners around Rio after the stadium’s operators rejected a request to host one of Vasco’s matches, citing, among other things, the condition of the grass.

The complaint was wonderfully simple: respect the rules. Vasco supporters argued that the concession requires equal treatment among Rio’s major clubs and prioritizes official football matches. Flamengo and Fluminense maintain that Vasco’s access is limited and subject to availability and technical conditions.

And now the Dallas Cowboys are coming.

There is something almost elegant about this.

Vasco cannot have the Maracanã because the field requires protection.

The NFL can have the Maracanã while emergency repairs are being discussed to make the field suitable for the NFL.

I do not offer this as evidence of conspiracy. Conspiracies generally require considerably more organization.

It is simply what happens to important places.

Everyone develops a very persuasive reason why his particular use of them should take precedence.

The Maracanã has spent most of its life being subjected to this kind of possessive grammar. My club. Our stadium. Their concession. The people’s ground. Brazil’s temple. Rio’s monument.

The nouns change. The claim does not.

Even ownership here is unusually theatrical.

The stadium belongs to the state. Flamengo and Fluminense hold the concession to operate it. Vasco has negotiated limited access while objecting when that access proves, in practice, limited.

This is less a property arrangement than a family WhatsApp group with lawyers.

And yet Sunday will look extraordinarily simple.

The Cowboys will be designated the home team. Baltimore and Dallas will enter the stadium. The NFL shield will appear everywhere it is contractually capable of appearing. The game will kick off at 5:25 in the afternoon local time. Ricky Martin and Pedro Sampaio are scheduled for halftime.

For approximately three hours, the building will acquire a temporary identity.

This happens frequently at famous places.

Wembley can become an NFL stadium. The Foro Italico can become a concert venue. The Grand Palais can become a fencing hall. A sufficiently desirable building eventually stops having one function and becomes something closer to cultural real estate.

The mistake is assuming this makes the place neutral.

It does the opposite.

Every new occupant arrives because of everything that happened there before him.

The NFL did not choose an anonymous 78,000-seat stadium in Rio. It chose the Maracanã.

That name arrives with its own weather system.

Two World Cup finals. The 2016 Olympic ceremonies. Flamengo. Fluminense. Vasco. Brazil. Uruguay. Pelé. Ghiggia. Generations of Cariocas with stories about particular sections, particular nights and particular ways home.

You cannot rent that history.

You can only rent the building underneath it.

This distinction tends to become clearer when someone new arrives.

There will be NFL fans in the stands Sunday. Many will be Brazilian. Some will have followed the Cowboys or Ravens for years. American football in Brazil does not require the audience to be explained into existence; the league has already played two regular-season games in São Paulo and returned because there is, quite plainly, a market. Rio is the new city, not Brazil the new country.

So this need not be framed as a visitation from a foreign civilization.

Rio has seen foreigners.

It survived Madonna.

The more interesting spectacle is watching an institution as confident as the NFL enter a place whose identity is even more secure.

American professional football is accustomed to transforming buildings around itself. Stadium clocks change. Advertising changes. Locker rooms change. Field markings change. A place is made legible to the league.

The Maracanã is unlikely to become legible quite so easily.

Its disputes will still be there Monday.

Flamengo will still believe it requires the building. Fluminense will still possess contractual rights inside it. Vasco supporters will still have opinions about equitable access. The state will still own it. And the grass, barring an unexpected spiritual recovery during the fourth quarter, will still need replacing.

This may be the Maracanã’s defining quality.

It accommodates everybody without quite surrendering to anyone.

The more people claim it, the less convincing any single claim becomes.

Perhaps that is why the condition of the field feels so wonderfully appropriate.

A stadium can absorb mythology more easily than it can absorb usage.

There is a limit to how many matches can be scheduled, how many events can be sold, how many institutions can announce that their need is exceptional.

Eventually the abstraction becomes physical.

The turf tears.

The seams show.

Someone has to stop talking about heritage and get the groundskeeper.

On Sunday, Lamar Jackson may scramble across it. Dak Prescott may throw touchdowns over it. Derrick Henry may introduce portions of it to the basic principles of force.

Millions of people will watch.

And for one afternoon the NFL will have the Maracanã entirely to itself.

Which is about as long as anyone ever does.
